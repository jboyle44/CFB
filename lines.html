How this works tap to expand

Every Tuesday, a script pulls that week's games, final scores, and market spreads from CollegeFootballData.com, plus MPG's power ratings for that week. It computes a model spread for every game and compares it to the market:

model_spread = (home_rating − away_rating) + HFA HFA = +3.0 pts for the home team, 0 on a neutral site edge = model_spread − vegas_spread pick = home team if edge > 0, away team if edge < 0

Once a game is final, the pick is graded straight against the market line that was live when it was made — that line is then frozen, so results here don't get rewritten as future lines move. A push means the actual margin landed exactly on the spread and doesn't count for or against the record.

Confidence stars. Every pick also gets a 1–5 star rating, based purely on the size of the edge (how far the model's line sits from the market's):

stars = 5 if |edge| ≥ 8.0 stars = 4 if |edge| ≥ 5.5 stars = 3 if |edge| ≥ 3.5 stars = 2 if |edge| ≥ 2.0 stars = 1 otherwise

These cutoffs aren't arbitrary round numbers — they're set from CFB's actual edge distribution so far this season (median edge 3.4 points, 90th percentile 8.0 points), roughly splitting picks into quintiles rather than piling everything into one or two star ratings. As of the last check, the real spread across a full slate of graded picks was 27 one-star, 34 two-star, 23 three-star, 22 four-star, and 12 five-star — a genuine bell-shaped spread, not a scale where every game happens to land at the same rating. Since a star rating is only ever computed from the edge, and the edge is frozen the moment a game kicks off, the star shown for a past pick can't drift or get revised after the fact either — it's a permanent record of how confident the model actually was at the time. The NFL page uses its own thresholds tuned to that market's tighter spreads, since NFL edges run smaller across the board than CFB's.

Why raw points, not a percentage of the spread. It's reasonable to wonder whether a 7-point edge should count for more on a 7-point spread than on a 40-point spread, the same way a 7-point edge matters more relative to a small number than a large one. That intuition holds in some sports — it's real in college basketball, where blowout games measurably get less predictable due to garbage time. But a study measuring ATS outcome variance against spread size across thousands of games found essentially no relationship in either football code — a correlation of -0.06 in the NFL and +0.09 in college football, both statistically negligible, versus -0.33 in college basketball where the effect is real. In other words, a given edge appears to carry about the same predictive weight whether the underlying spread is small or enormous, at least in football. That's why stars here are based on the raw edge rather than the edge scaled by spread size.
